# Deep-column workflow

## Start from a falsifiable question

Turn a broad theme into a question that can be answered with public evidence.

Weak:

> AI is driving a data-center boom.

Better:

> Which disclosed constraints determine whether announced AI compute demand becomes energized high-density capacity in the next 12–36 months?

Define geography, time horizon, infrastructure layer, and publication cutoff.

## Build a question tree

Use four to seven branches:

1. What changed?
2. Is it demand, supply, timing, price, mix, or accounting?
3. Which physical and commercial links transmit the change?
4. Which primary sources independently confirm it?
5. What alternative explanation fits the facts?
6. What is not yet observable?
7. What future disclosure would settle the debate?

Keep the tree bounded. Remove branches that do not affect the conclusion.

## Construct an evidence map

Organize evidence by claim, not by source:

| Claim | Supporting primary evidence | Counter-evidence | Status | Time horizon |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Use company filings, grid and government records, project-owner disclosures, permits, procurement records, customer or supplier announcements, and official technical documentation. Media may identify a lead but cannot complete an unsupported claim.

## Analyze mechanisms, not correlations

For an Atlas column, a useful mechanism normally contains:

1. **Trigger** — the disclosure or observable change.
2. **Transmission** — the physical, contractual, or financial path.
3. **Gate** — power, grid, land, financing, equipment, qualification, construction, cooling, or customer demand.
4. **Timing** — immediate, 6–12 months, 12–36 months, or longer.
5. **Observable test** — the next filing, project milestone, shipment, permit, or capacity event that would confirm it.

Treat correlation as a clue, not causation.

## Use a four-pass internal review

The passes are roles, not fictional quotations:

1. **Evidence analyst** — verifies sources, dates, definitions, and calculations.
2. **Mechanism analyst** — tests every causal link and time lag.
3. **Skeptical reviewer** — develops the strongest alternative explanation and searches for disconfirming evidence.
4. **Editor** — removes repetition, weak claims, generic background, and unsupported certainty.

Do not publish the hidden reasoning. Publish the evidence, conclusion, uncertainty, and counter-case.

## Recommended article structure

1. **Title** — precise and non-promotional.
2. **Factual lead** — the strongest new disclosure or contradiction.
3. **Why this matters** — the Atlas question, not a market slogan.
4. **Evidence map** — two to five decisive facts.
5. **Mechanism** — show how the change travels through the infrastructure chain.
6. **Bottleneck and timing** — distinguish announced, contracted, under-construction, and operational states.
7. **Counter-case** — explain the best competing interpretation.
8. **Atlas conclusion** — scoped, dated, and conditional.
9. **What to watch** — observable events that would confirm or overturn it.
10. **Source ledger** — dated clickable primary links.

Use a comparison table, timeline, or small mechanism diagram only when it materially improves understanding.

## Writing standards

- Lead with evidence, not atmosphere.
- Replace “significant”, “strong”, and “huge” with a number or remove them.
- Keep one analytical job per paragraph.
- Separate company claims from independently verified facts.
- Preserve negative findings and no-change results.
- State when different sources measure different universes.
- Explain why a number matters before adding another number.
- Avoid generic company histories unless they change the mechanism.
- Do not write a security recommendation.

## Column completion test

A deep column is ready only if a skeptical reader can answer:

- What exactly is new?
- Which primary sources support it?
- What is fact versus management claim versus Atlas inference?
- How does the mechanism work?
- Where can it fail?
- Over what time horizon?
- What evidence would change the conclusion?
